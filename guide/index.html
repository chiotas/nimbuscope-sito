Quick start

How to read the sky with NimbuScope, from your spot to the verdict.

Download the PDF
1

The question

“Tomorrow at sunset, from that position, will the sunlight get through?” NimbuScope follows the sun’s ray from your spot, minute by minute and kilometre by kilometre, through the clouds the weather model forecasts, to try to answer this question. The clouds that block the light are often very far away, and it isn’t easy to see how they interact with the light, but NimbuScope makes it easy.

2

Your spot

Use your location, search for a place in the bar at the top, or hold a finger on the map to move the point wherever you want. Next to the name is your elevation: your height above sea level, where you shoot from. If you stand on a cliff or a terrace, tap the elevation and correct it. A higher spot sees farther.

3

Read the sky in four places

  1. Your spot, at the top.
  2. The sun line and the rings. Each dashed ring is the distance where the ray reaches a cloud layer: L low, M mid, H high.
  3. The verdict, next to the big time.
  4. The light window, in gold under the bar: the minutes in a row with direct light.
4

Every verdict

  • Nothing blocking: the ray meets no cloud base that stops it. The line below says what it passes: Under the low clouds, Through the high clouds.
  • Blocked at 7 km: the ray stops in a cloud layer, at that distance in a straight line.
  • Blocked here: you are inside the layer.
  • Behind the ridge: the sun is behind the mountains.
  • Below the horizon: the sun isn’t up yet, or it has set.
  • Fog and Mist: you are in it. Fog below you means you are above it.
  • Unknown: the ray leaves the area the model covers before it climbs above the clouds.
  • Above the ridge: no cloud forecast for this day yet; only the sun and the mountains.
5

Slide through the minutes

Slide the bar and the verdict, the map and Viewpoint follow, minute by minute. The ▶︎ button runs the minutes for you. Under the bar you find two times. Sunset is the standard time every app gives. Visible is the real time from your spot, when the sun disappears behind the mountains. At sea they are often the same.

6

Viewpoint

Tap Viewpoint for the scene from your spot at the same minute. Panorama shows what you see in front of you: the mountains, the sun’s path, the clouds at their real distance. Section shows why: a cut of the sky along the ray, from your feet to the sun. Heights are stretched, and the drawing says by how much.

7

Where the clouds come from

Tap the model’s name on the map, for example UKV. You see which model you are using, when it last updated and when the next run arrives. Models update every three hours. In the evening you can still look back at that morning’s sunrise.

8

Three things that confuse at first

  • It’s about direct light, not colour. NimbuScope doesn’t promise colour (for now): it’s up to us to read the situation, because a block doesn’t necessarily mean no colour.
  • The dashed rings are an estimate, worked out from typical cloud heights. The solid arc is the base that really blocks the ray.
  • “Sunset” and “Visible” are two different times. In the mountains the sun can disappear many minutes before sunset.

How I use it

The tutorial in the app shows you the map, the Panorama and the Section. Here is how I use them myself to decide, before heading out to shoot. These are my habits, not rules, and in the end remember that nature is always in charge.

1

Before heading out for sunset

I open Times and rings and look at three things. The rings tell me where the clouds that count are: at Kynance, at an elevation of 2 m, I have the low ones between 36 and 120 km, the mid ones between 120 and 241. The verdict changes minute by minute and tells me whether those clouds will block the light or not. And above all it tells me the light window: twenty minutes of direct light are worth the alarm clock, two minutes are a gamble. If my spot is blocked, I move the point along the coast and look again.

2

The morning, still in bed :)

In the morning, before getting out of bed, I check whether the new forecast run has arrived and what has changed. A verdict that holds run after run is worth more than a good one that has just appeared. In the UK I compare UKV and ICON-EU in the ⓘ sheet. When they agree, I trust them more. When they don’t, I try to work out with the satellite which one is more realistic.

3

On the spot, an hour before

I’m on the spot at least an hour before. I open Viewpoint. The Section tells me whether the light can pass under the low clouds, and the bar for how many minutes: when the sun is low and the ray runs under their base, that’s when they can light up. The Panorama tells me where the sun will touch the horizon, among the real mountains and islands. So I compose before the light arrives, not while it’s there.

Example 1

Kynance Cove, 20 November

A forecast, not a check of what happened. At 15:00 the app says Blocked at 7 km: a mid layer with rain forecast, base 2,630 m, right in front of me. I don’t stop at the first verdict and slide the bar. From 16:12 the sun is low enough for the ray to run under the low clouds, whose base is at 1,180 m, and the light window lasts until sunset at 16:31. I’d be on the rocks by half past three, with the composition ready, for the nineteen minutes of direct light the forecast gives me under the low clouds.

Example 2

Portwrinkle, 8 November

A tester’s spot on the south coast of Cornwall, again a forecast. Direct light until 16:21, then Blocked at 162 km: a mid bank far out on the sun’s line, base 5,250 m, rising to 7,800 m. The direct light ends about twenty minutes before the 16:42 sunset. But the Section also shows high clouds above the bank, the pink layer in the drawing. So I wouldn’t leave at 16:21: I’d stay for the minutes after sunset, when the high clouds can light up from below.

4

What I ask you, as a tester

You are among the first to use NimbuScope, and what you see on the spot is worth more than any test of mine.

  • Choose a sunrise or a sunset where you’ll be in the next few days, and read it the evening before and in the morning.
  • Before you leave, take a screenshot of the app (the map or the Section), with the time on it.
  • On the spot, take a photo of the sky at that time, even with your phone.
  • Reply to the email this guide came with: place, time, screenshot, photo, and what surprised you. Please don’t use TestFlight: by email everything reaches me in one place, from iPhone and Android.

Tell me too where the app confused you.
